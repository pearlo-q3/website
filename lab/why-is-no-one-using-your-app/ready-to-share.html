Ready to share: is your app ready for real users?

By Pearl Oguchi · Updated 27 November 2026

Short answer

If the only people who've used your app are friends, family or your own team, you don't know yet whether real users will sign up and come back. The most reliable way to find out is to watch someone who's never seen it try the live product with no help from you. If you've got nobody to watch, you can still find out a lot on your own: go through it the way a new user would arrive, count the "how do I…?" questions, and put a small cold ad in front of people who've never heard of you. Skip this, and the first people you share it with often sign up, don't come back, and you never find out why.

Friends are polite. That's the problem.

I hear this a lot: "My friends love it." "We showed it to a few people and they said it was great." I believe them. I just don't believe it tells you much.

Friends and family are being kind, not honest. They know you've put months into this. Nobody wants to be the one who says the sign-up form is confusing, or that they couldn't work out what to tap next. So they say it's great, they mean well, and you walk away thinking you've validated something.

Real validation is usage or payment. Did they come back without you asking them to? Did they pay for it, or would they, with their own money? If the answer to both is no, you haven't tested the product. You've tested whether people like you.

Share the published app, not a preview only you can see

This one catches good founders out. I went through this with someone recently who'd spent a week gathering feedback from early testers. The feedback was mixed, some of it quite negative, and he was starting to doubt the whole direction.

Then it came out that the testers hadn't opened his actual, published app. They'd been looking at a preview link, one only he could see, that didn't match what a real user would land on. Every note they'd given him was about something his real users would never come across.

It's an easy mistake to make. You're moving fast, you share whatever's in front of you, and you forget that "what I'm looking at" and "what they'll get" aren't always the same thing. Whichever of the ways below you use, make sure it's the real, live product.

Your team says it works. So why don't people stick?

Teams test for bugs. Does it crash, does the button work, does the form submit. That's necessary, and it's a different question from "will real users stick with it?"

Software can work perfectly and still fail the moment it meets someone with no patience and no reason to try twice. A login that technically works but asks for six fields before it lets you look around. A results screen that's accurate but makes no sense without context you never gave them. Nothing's broken. It's just not ready.

This is where I hear founders ask, "How do we get people to stick?" Or they tell me people sign up, don't get anything from it, and "they delete it and they move on." Nothing crashed. They just couldn't see a reason to stay.

Ready means someone who's never seen it, with no explanation from you, can sign up, understand what just happened and want to come back. So how do you find out?

Way 1: Watch someone use it

If you can find even one person who's never used your product, this is still the best test there is. You don't need a lab or a research budget. You need that person, a few minutes, and the discipline to say nothing while they try it.

Hand them your live app or site with no instructions. Watch where they hesitate. Watch where they stop and look at you, waiting to be told what to do. Don't fill the silence. That hesitation is the data.

Way 2: No testers yet? Be the new user yourself

Plenty of founders don't have a single person to try it yet. That's fine. Start with a question I ask on almost every call: do you use your own app, regularly, the way a real user would? Not to check a feature works. To actually get something done with it.

If you don't, you're asking real users to put up with friction you've never felt. So go through it properly, as if you'd never seen it:

  • Start with an empty account. Most founders test with an account full of their own data. A new user's first look is an empty app. Create a brand new account and see what it shows when there's nothing in it. Blank dashboards and "no results" screens send people away fast.
  • Arrive the way a new user would. Don't type in the link you know. Start from a Google search, your app store listing or your landing page. Use a phone you don't build on and a new email address. Every time you think "I'd know to do this because I built it", write it down.
  • Step off the happy path. New users hit the edges. Check the verification email doesn't land in spam. Try the password reset. Press back halfway through sign-up. Type something wrong. A lot of "nobody comes back" turns out to be "nobody got in".
  • Read only the words on the screen. Go screen by screen and ask: would someone who's never heard my pitch understand this button, this label, this heading? Mark every word that only makes sense if you've spoken to the founder.

I use my CRM every single day. A while back I realised it had about 200 contacts in it and I couldn't tell which ones were leads and which were people I'd actually spoken to. Nothing was broken. The software did exactly what it was built to do. It expected me to convert a lead once I'd spoken to them, and I never did, because nothing on the screen made that step feel like something I needed to do. So the mess built up quietly, and I only noticed because I was in it daily.

That's what your users go through. They won't email you to say they missed a step. They'll just slowly stop getting value, and drift. If you're using your own product every day, you'll feel that before they leave. If you're not, you won't.

Way 3: Count the "how do I…?" questions

Every time someone asks "how do I…?", that's a place where the product didn't explain itself.

Go back through your inbox, DMs, WhatsApp messages, comments on your launch post, and the questions people asked when you showed it to them, friends included. If you've had no one yet, use your own list from Way 2. Write each question down next to the screen it was about.

When the same question turns up twice, fix the screen. Don't write a longer help page. People rarely read it, and a new user won't email you to ask. They'll just leave.

Way 4: Run a small cold ad to your landing page

Before a new user can get stuck in your app, they have to decide it's worth trying. You can test that step without knowing a single user.

Put a small ad, on a budget you'd be comfortable losing, in front of people who match who you built it for but have never heard of you. Send them to your landing page. Then look at three things: do they click, do they sign up, and do the ones who sign up get to the first useful thing?

If nobody signs up, the problem starts before the app, on the landing page. Think Ronseal: does the product do what it says on the tin? If they sign up and then stall, go back to Way 2 and find the screen where they stopped.

Common questions

Is my app ready for real users?

Only if someone who's never seen it can use the live, published version alone and come out the other side understanding what it does and wanting to come back. If you haven't seen that happen, or tested for it yourself, you don't know yet.

Is feedback from friends and family enough?

No. They're being kind, not honest, and politeness isn't validation. Real validation is usage or payment: did they come back unprompted, or would they pay with their own money?

How do I test my app if I don't have any users yet?

Be the new user yourself. Create a brand new, empty account, arrive from a Google search or your landing page rather than a link you know, try the edges like password reset and verification emails, and read every screen as if you'd never heard your own pitch. Write down every point where you only knew what to do because you built it.

Why do people sign up and then delete the app?

Usually because they didn't get anything useful the first time they tried it. Create a new, empty account, go through it the way a new user would, and find the screen where you'd give up if you hadn't built it.

How do I find out if people who don't know me will even try my app?

Run a small cold ad, on a budget you're happy to lose, to people who match your audience but don't know you, and send them to your landing page. If they don't sign up, the problem starts on the landing page. If they sign up and stall, look at your first few screens.

What's the difference between my team saying it's stable and it actually being ready?

Your team tests for bugs: does it crash, does it save, does it load. A new user is testing something else: can they work out what to do with no help and no context. Stable and ready for real users aren't the same thing.

What if the feedback I've already collected was negative? Should I start over?

Not necessarily. Check first what they were actually testing. Feedback on a preview link, an old build or a version only you can see tells you nothing about your real product. Confirm they used the live thing before you act on what they said.

Self-check

Whichever way you use, ask yourself honestly: would a new user have got this far on their own? Not "did it work". Would they have stayed?
